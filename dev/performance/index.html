    Performance¶

    Tack's visualization filters against VTK and Viskores, on the CPU and the GPU, on synthetic meshes and on real simulation data. Every Tack output is checked against VTK's before its time counts: counts, sums of positions and values, areas and volumes.

    Each entry below is how many times faster Tack is: the other system's time over Tack's, so above 1× Tack is faster. Tack's time is its fresh time, which includes deriving the connectivity it needs (faces, point links) from a mesh it has not seen, since VTK and Viskores rebuild theirs on every call. The running times, fresh and warm, are at the bottom.

    How these were measured¶

    • One process per comparison. Tack runs in the same process as VTK (the CPU) or Viskores (the GPU), on the same mesh, and its output is checked against the other's before any timing.
    • Viskores through VTK: its filters are VTK's Viskores filters (vtkmContour and the others). On the GPU it was handed Tack's device arrays and kept its output on the device, and a trace of every run confirmed no transfers beyond sizes and counts. It ran with cudaMalloc (NO_VISKORES_MANAGED_MEMORY=1): its default, cudaMallocManaged, was 4.7–25× slower from page faults on new buffers.
    • Warm and fresh. A warm Tack run reuses the connectivity its topology keeps from an earlier filter on the same mesh; a fresh one derives it.
    • Timing. Repeated runs for a fixed budget, the median taken; Tack's timing ends with tack.sync(), since GPU launches are queued. On the Linux machine, two passes: the VTK and Tack columns take the better of the two, the Viskores column their mean (the second pass in reverse order); on the Mac, one.
    • Harness: benchmarks/vtk_compare (VTK and Tack) and benchmarks/viskores_compare (Viskores and Tack), on the meshes of benchmarks/vtk_compare/meshes.py. Point gradients against VTK (vtkGradientFilter) are a pair of vtk_compare since 0.6.1's run (#195).

    Notes¶

    • Metal GPU vs CUDA GPU are in the same ballpark (~19-29 G elem/s) despite very different architectures. Both are memory-bandwidth limited for simple kernels, compute-limited for transcendentals.

    • CPU/NumPy gap between Mac and Linux is mostly memory bandwidth: Apple Silicon unified memory delivers ~100-200 GB/s vs ~4 GB/s effective on this DDR3 Xeon box. The CPU itself (Sandy Bridge 2012 @ 2.0 GHz, AVX1 only) also contributes to the gap.

    • FILL hits 62 G elem/s on CUDA (write-only, no reads) — close to the 4060 Ti's theoretical 288 GB/s (62G × 4 bytes = 248 GB/s).

    • sin/exp show the largest CUDA-vs-CPU speedup (34-45x) because CUDA's Special Function Units (SFUs) compute transcendentals in hardware.

    • reduce favors Metal (39G vs 26G) — likely benefiting from unified memory avoiding a writeback path for partial sums.
