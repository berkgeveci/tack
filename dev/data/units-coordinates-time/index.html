    Coordinates, units and time¶

    A coordinate millions of metres from zero loses its fine digits in single precision. A temperature means little until you know whether it is in kelvin or in degrees Celsius, and a simulation's step means little without its time. So besides its cells and fields, a Tack dataset records three facts: where its positions are measured from, the units of its positions and fields, and when the data are. VTK keeps a unit in an array's UNITS_LABEL and a time in DATA_TIME_STEP. Tack keeps them on the field and on the dataset, and filters keep them through a pipeline.

    A survey far from zero, in metres, at one time¶

    The example holds three LiDAR returns in projected coordinates, millions of metres from the datum. It gives the positions a unit, gives the temperature field a unit, and stamps the dataset with a time. Single precision keeps about seven digits, so it cannot hold the quarter metre in 4,580,000.25. The dataset keeps it anyway.

    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

world = np.array([[4580000.25, 512000.50, 10.0],       # metres, in double precision
                  [4580010.00, 512005.00, 12.0],
                  [4580020.75, 512010.25, 11.5]])
cloud = td.point_cloud(world, fields={"temperature": [12.5, 12.0, 11.8]},
                       unit="m", units={"temperature": "degC"})
cloud.time, cloud.time_unit = 3600.0, "s"

print(f"{np.float32(world[0, 0]):.2f}")  # 4580000.00: single precision drops the quarter metre
print(cloud.dtype, cloud.origin)        # tack.f32 (4580000.0, 512000.0, 10.0)
print(cloud.positions()[0])             # [0.25 0.5  0.  ]: single precision, from the origin
print(cloud.unit, cloud.fields["temperature"].unit, cloud.time, cloud.time_unit)   # m °C 3600.0 s

assert np.array_equal(cloud.world_positions(), world)    # every digit kept

    The positions are stored in single precision, but relative to an origin that is held in double precision. Their sum, world_positions(), is the input exactly. The next sections explain the origin, the units and the time.

    Positions are relative to an origin¶

    A dataset's origin is three double-precision numbers kept on the host. Its geometry stores each position relative to the origin, in the geometry's own type, which is often single precision. Kernels see only these relative values. Since they are small, single precision keeps the detail that matters: the spacing between returns, a cell's thin edge, a gradient's small differences.

    A dataset gives you its positions both ways:

    • data.positions() returns the stored values, relative to the origin.
    • data.world_positions() adds the origin in double precision and returns positions in the world's coordinates.

    An origin of zero, the default, makes the two the same. You can set one with DataSet(..., origin=). point_cloud chooses one when it is given double-precision positions far from zero for their size. It uses the lower corner of their box, rounded down to whole units, as in the example.

    An image keeps its own corner in its UniformCoordinates, in the geometry's type. So a raster placed at survey coordinates loses digits in single precision. rebased(origin) moves the image's corner into the dataset's origin, in double precision, and keeps it an image:

    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

raster = td.uniform_grid((4, 3), origin=(4580000.0, 512000.0, 0.0), spacing=(0.1, 0.1), unit="m")
print(raster.world_positions()[1])     # [4580000.  512000.  0.]: the 0.1 m is lost

near = raster.rebased((4580000.0, 512000.0, 0.0))
print(near.positions()[1])             # [0.1 0.  0. ]
print(type(near.geometry.values).__name__)                  # UniformCoordinates
assert abs(near.world_positions()[1, 0] - 4580000.1) < 1e-6

    Filters keep their input's origin in what they return. Anything you give a filter in world coordinates, such as a probe point, a seed or the centre of an implicit sphere, is moved to the dataset's origin in double precision first. Datasets whose positions meet, such as the pieces of one mesh, share one origin.

    Units on fields and positions¶

    A field's unit is a string such as "K", "m/s" or "kg/m**3", given as Field(space, values, unit=). The spatial unit, data.unit, is the unit of the positions and of the origin. It belongs to the geometry field, and the grid and point cloud constructors take it as unit=. Arrays have no unit, and kernels never see one. A unit is meaning, kept beside the values.

    None means the unit is unknown, and it is the default. It is not the same as "dimensionless", the unit of a pure number. Nothing derived from an unknown unit is guessed, so a gradient of a field of unknown unit has an unknown unit too.

    Tack parses and compares units with the unyt library, and stores each in unyt's spelling. So "m/s", "m*s**-1" and "m/s^1" are one unit, and a string that is not a unit is refused when the field is made. td.units has the helpers you need to work with them:

    import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

print(td.units.canonical("m/s^1"))              # m/s
print(td.units.canonical("degC"))               # °C
print(td.units.conversion("degC", "K"))         # (1.0, 273.15): K = 1.0 * °C + 273.15
print(td.units.same("km", "1000*m"))            # True
print(td.units.derived(("K", 1), ("m", -1)))    # K/m, the unit of a temperature gradient

data = td.uniform_grid((3, 2, 2), unit="km")
data.fields["density"] = td.Field(td.H1(data), tack.ones((data.num_points,)), unit="kg/m^3")
assert data.unit == "km" and data.fields["density"].unit == "kg/m**3"

    Simulation code units are scaled units, such as "3.0857e21*cm" for a kiloparsec in centimetres, and need nothing registered. Besides unyt's units, Tack knows the US survey foot, "ftUS", which State Plane coordinates and older LiDAR use.

    What filters do with units¶

    Units matter most in the Visualization layer, which this page only previews. A filter that keeps a quantity keeps its unit, so a contour's carried temperature is still in kelvin. A filter that computes a new quantity derives its unit, so a gradient of temperature is in kelvin per metre. A difference of Celsius temperatures is in kelvin. Parameters may be quantities, such as a radius of "10 cm" on a mesh in metres. Positions in two different known units are refused where they meet. td.convert(data, unit) scales a field or the positions to another unit. The units reference lists every rule.

    A dataset's time¶

    DataSet(..., time=, time_unit=) records when the data are. Both may also be set later, as data.time and data.time_unit. The time is one double-precision number, or None. The time unit must be a unit of time, such as "s" or "ms", or None when it is unknown.

    A dataset is one step of a simulation, not a series. Tack keeps no collection of time steps, as a VTK reader's time steps would be. Filters keep their input's time, so a step's results carry its time through a pipeline. Filters that need earlier steps, such as a running average or particle paths, keep what they need from each step you give them. Steps over time describes them. The pieces of one partitioned dataset are at one time.

    Details¶

    Converting the positions. Converting the spatial unit converts the origin too, in double precision. Convert through the dataset, with td.convert(data, "m"), not by converting the geometry field alone, which would leave the origin in the old unit.

    Offset units. Degrees Celsius and Fahrenheit have a zero that is not zero. Tack does not multiply them, so a product of a Celsius field with another is refused until you convert to kelvin.

    VTK. The VTK interop reads and writes each array's UNITS_LABEL and the dataset's DATA_TIME_STEP. VTK has no time unit and nowhere to keep an image's spatial unit, so those are lost on the way to VTK. A label that is not a unit is dropped with a warning. See VTK.

    Cost. unyt is imported the first time a unit is given, so a program that gives none pays nothing for it.
