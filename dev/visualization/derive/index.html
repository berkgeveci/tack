    Derive quantities¶

    Many questions about a simulation are about quantities it did not store: the gradient of a pressure, the vorticity of a flow, the volume and shape of each cell, the total mass in a region, a cell field seen at the points. Tack computes each of them with a filter that has a VTK counterpart: vtkGradientFilter, vtkCellSizeFilter, vtkMeshQuality, vtkIntegrateAttributes and vtkCellDataToPointData. Every result comes with its layout and its unit. A field can move between point and cell data in more than one way, and the last section helps you choose.

    The filters here return fields or numbers, not a new dataset. Put a field in data.fields to keep it with the dataset, so that later filters carry it.

    Compute gradients and flow quantities¶

    gradients(data, field) is VTK's vtkGradientFilter. By default it gives the gradient at each cell's centre. With at="points" it gives each cell's gradient at each point, averaged over the cells around the point, as VTK does. flow_quantities derives the divergence, the vorticity and the Q-criterion from a velocity's gradient.

    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

data = td.uniform_grid((11, 11, 11), origin=(-1, -1, -1), spacing=(0.2, 0.2, 0.2), unit="m")
x = data.positions()
data.fields["T"] = td.Field(td.H1(data), tack.array(300 + 10 * x[:, 0]), unit="K")
rotation = np.stack([-x[:, 1], x[:, 0], np.zeros(len(x), np.float32)], axis=1)
data.fields["velocity"] = td.Field(td.H1(data), tack.array(rotation, dtype=tack.vec3(tack.f32)),
                                   unit="m/s")

grad_T = td.gradients(data, "T")                         # one 3-vector per cell
at_points = td.gradients(data, "T", at="points")         # one per point
flow = td.flow_quantities(data, td.gradients(data, "velocity"))

print(grad_T.values.numpy()[0].round(3), grad_T.unit)              # [10. 0. 0.] K/m
print(flow.vorticity.values.numpy()[0].round(3), flow.vorticity.unit)  # [0. 0. 2.] 1/s
assert np.allclose(at_points.values.numpy(), [10, 0, 0], atol=1e-4)
assert np.allclose(flow.divergence.values.numpy(), 0, atol=1e-5)
assert np.allclose(flow.q_criterion.values.numpy(), 1, atol=1e-5)

    The field may be point data (an H1 field of order 1), cell data (a Constant field), or a field of any order, continuous (H1) or discontinuous (L2). The gradient is computed through the field's own interpolation in each cell, and the geometry's Jacobian turns it into a gradient in space.

    • Layout. A scalar's gradient is a 3-vector. A vector of k components has 3k values, row by row: d(u_i)/d(x_j) is at 3 * i + j. That is VTK's and Viskores' layout of the gradient tensor, and what flow_quantities reads.
    • Units. The gradient's unit is the field's over the spatial unit: K/m above. A field or a dataset without a unit gives a gradient without one.
    • Higher order. at may also be an L2 space of the dataset: td.gradients(data, "u", at=td.L2(data, order=p)). That gives the gradient at every node of every cell, exactly, without the averaging that at="points" does. An order-p field keeps its detail this way.
    • 2D and degenerate cells. A 2D cell's gradient lies in its plane, as in VTK. Lines and vertices get zero, and a cell of no volume gets NaN.
    • Polyhedra. A polyhedron has no reference element, so its gradient of point data is computed by Green-Gauss over its faces. That is exact for a linear field.

    flow_quantities returns a named tuple of three fields, divergence, vorticity and q_criterion, in the gradient's space: per cell from a cell gradient, per point from a point gradient. See the reference for gradients and flow_quantities.

    Other fields from fields¶

    Smaller filters compute a field from fields pointwise. Each returns a field in its input's space, with a derived unit.

    Tack Gives Viskores
    magnitude(data, field) each vector's length VectorMagnitude
    dot(data, a, b) the dot product of two vector fields DotProduct
    cross(data, a, b) the cross product of two 3-vector fields CrossProduct
    composite(data, *fields) scalar fields as the components of a vector CompositeVectors
    log_values(data, field) the logarithm, in any base LogValues
    convert(data, unit, field) a field, or the geometry, in another unit

    dot, cross and composite need fields of one value type. VTK's vtkArrayCalculator has no counterpart: a formula of your own is a short kernel, as write a mesh algorithm shows.

    Measure cells¶

    cell_geometry(data) gives each cell's measure (volume, area or length) and its centroid, as VTK's vtkCellSizeFilter does, as a named tuple of two cell fields. A 3D cell's volume comes from the divergence theorem over its faces, so it works on every shape, polyhedra included. A curved cell is measured by quadrature through its own map, so its volume is the curved cell's.

    cell_centers(data) is each cell's parametric centre, as VTK's vtkCellCenters. It is not the centroid on a cell that is not affine, such as a skewed hexahedron. Use cell_geometry(data).centroid for the centroid.

    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

data = td.uniform_grid((11, 11, 11), origin=(-1, -1, -1), spacing=(0.2, 0.2, 0.2), unit="m")
cells = td.cell_geometry(data)
volume = cells.measure.values.numpy()
print(f"{volume.sum():.3f} {cells.measure.unit}")         # 8.000 m**3
assert np.isclose(volume.sum(), 8.0)
assert np.allclose(cells.centroid.values.numpy(), td.cell_centers(data).values.numpy())

faces = td.face_geometry(data)                             # each face's normal and area
assert np.isclose(faces.area.values.numpy().max(), 0.04)

    On faces and edges, face_geometry gives each face's unit normal and area, face_centers each face's centroid, and edge_lengths each edge's length. Their fields live on the faces and edges that data.topology.faces() and edges() number. See the reference for cell_geometry and cell_centers.

    Measure cell quality¶

    cell_quality(data, measures) says how well shaped each cell is: a dict of cell fields, one per measure. These are Verdict's definitions, as VTK's vtkMeshQuality uses them, and each measure is 1 on the ideal cell of its shape: an equilateral triangle, a square, a cube, a regular tetrahedron.

    Measure What it says
    scaled_jacobian the worst corner's edges' determinant, the edges scaled to unit length: 1 ideal, 0 flat, negative inverted
    jacobian_ratio the least corner determinant over the greatest: 1 for every simplex, below 1 as a cell tapers
    condition the worst corner's condition number: 1 ideal, growing as the cell distorts
    edge_ratio the longest edge over the shortest
    min_edge, max_edge the shortest and the longest edge, in the spatial unit 
    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

mesh = td.tetrahedralize(td.uniform_grid((4, 4, 4)))
quality = td.cell_quality(mesh, ["scaled_jacobian", "condition", "edge_ratio"])
sj = quality["scaled_jacobian"].values.numpy()
print(f"scaled Jacobian {sj.min():.3f} to {sj.max():.3f}")   # 0.707 to 1.000

mesh.fields["sj"] = quality["scaled_jacobian"]
worst = td.threshold(mesh, "sj", -1.0, 0.75)                 # the cells to look at
assert np.isclose(sj.max(), 1.0) and np.isclose(sj.min(), 1 / np.sqrt(2))
assert worst.num_cells == np.count_nonzero(sj < 0.75)

    The measures extend to every shape Tack has. Wedges and pyramids get every measure, and an order-2 cell is measured along its curved edges. Polyhedra and polygons are measured by the tetrahedra and triangles they are fanned into. Their Jacobian measures are then on the fan's scale, not the shape's: a cube stored as a polyhedron scores 0.58, not 1. Read them as a validity check (a positive scaled Jacobian means no fan tetrahedron is flat or inverted), and compare polyhedra with each other. The reference has the formulas and where VTK 9.7.1 differs.

    Integrate fields¶

    integrate(data) gives the mesh's measure (its total volume, area or length) and the integral of each field over it, as VTK's vtkIntegrateAttributes does. It returns an Integrals of measure, measure_unit, values and units. A vector field's integral is a tuple.

    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

data = td.uniform_grid((11, 11, 11), origin=(-1, -1, -1), spacing=(0.2, 0.2, 0.2), unit="m")
x = data.positions()
data.fields["T"] = td.Field(td.H1(data), tack.array(300 + 10 * x[:, 0]), unit="K")

totals = td.integrate(data, ["T"])
print(f"{totals.measure:.1f} {totals.measure_unit}, {totals.values['T']:.1f} {totals.units['T']}")
# 8.0 m**3, 2400.0 K*m**3
assert np.isclose(totals.measure, 8.0) and np.isclose(totals.values["T"], 2400.0, rtol=1e-6)
mean_T = totals.values["T"] / totals.measure            # the volume-weighted mean, 300 K
assert np.isclose(mean_T, 300.0, rtol=1e-6)

    Each cell is integrated by Gauss quadrature through its own map, with enough points for the field's order and the geometry's. A field of any order on a curved mesh therefore integrates exactly, to rounding. Only the cells of the mesh's dimension count, as in VTK: a volume's integrals leave out its surface cells. Ghost copies are left out, and over a PartitionedDataSet the pieces are summed over every rank.

    An integral is not a sum of samples. statistics gives the mean of the stored values, which on a graded mesh is not the volume-weighted mean that integrate gives (reduce explains the difference). See the reference.

    Move fields between point and cell data¶

    project(data, field, space) makes a new field of field in another space of the same dataset. It covers VTK's vtkCellDataToPointData and vtkPointDataToCellData, and more. The common targets:

    Target space Each value VTK
    td.H1(data) (point data) the mean of the source at the point, over the cells around it vtkCellDataToPointData
    td.Constant(data) (cell data) the source's average over the cell: its integral over the cell's measure
    td.Constant(data) with average="nodes" the mean of the source's values in the cell vtkPointDataToCellData
    td.L2(data, order=1) the source's values at the cell's corners 
    import numpy as np

import tack
import tack.dataset as td

tack.set_default_device("cpu")

data = td.uniform_grid((11, 11, 11), origin=(-1, -1, -1), spacing=(0.2, 0.2, 0.2))
x = data.positions()
data.fields["T"] = td.Field(td.H1(data), tack.array(300 + 10 * x[:, 0]))

per_cell = td.project(data, "T", td.Constant(data))          # each cell's average
per_point = td.project(data, per_cell, td.H1(data))          # back to the points
at_centres = td.values_at_centers(data, "T")                 # T at each cell's centre

print(per_cell.values.numpy()[:3])                           # [291. 293. 295.]
assert np.allclose(per_cell.values.numpy(), at_centres.values.numpy())
interior = (np.abs(x[:, 0]) < 0.9)                           # away from the ends
assert np.allclose(per_point.values.numpy()[interior], data.fields["T"].values.numpy()[interior])

    The two averages into cell data differ in what they mean. The default, average="integral", is the field's true average over the cell, exact for any order and any curved cell. average="nodes" is VTK's mean of the values the cell stores. The two agree for a linear field on a tetrahedron or a box, but not on a pyramid, a distorted cell or a field of higher order.

    Into point data, a continuous field comes across exactly, and where cells disagree (cell data, or a discontinuous field) each point takes their mean. Values on faces or edges have no interpolation, so they cannot be projected.

    values_at_centers(data, field) evaluates a field at each cell's parametric centre through its interpolation. For a linear field that equals the cell average, as in the example. For higher-order fields it is a point value, not an average. See the reference for project and values_at_centers. The fields page explains spaces, and when each projection is the right one.

    Details¶

    Precision. Filters compute in the dataset's precision, which vtk_to_dataset makes single by default. Jacobians and gradients are formed from differences of corners and values, never from absolute positions, so thin cells keep their short edges' accuracy in single precision. Where double precision matters, read the data with dtype=tack.f64 on a device that has it.

    Singular corners. At the points, a cell whose Jacobian is singular at a corner (where two faces meet flat) has no gradient there. It is left out of that point's average, which is NaN only if no cell around the point is left. A pyramid's apex gets the gradient from just below it, as VTK's vtkPyramid does.

    Cells without a reference element. On polyhedra, polygons and polylines, project moves only point data and cell data, one into the other. values_at_centers and cell_centers use the cell point, the mean of the cell's distinct points, which is where the cell's interpolation is fanned from. The polyhedra page explains why.
